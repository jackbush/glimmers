# Atlas Nights: sky cultures

What each choice in the Sky culture menu is, where it comes from, and how it was put together.
The data is built by [`scripts/sky-data.mjs`](../../../scripts/sky-data.mjs) into `sky.js`;
stars everywhere come from the [Yale Bright Star Catalogue](http://tdc-www.harvard.edu/catalogs/bsc5.html)
(public domain), placed for 31.6°N.

## Bedouin (the default)

The sky of the Arabian Peninsula before Greek astronomy reached it in the 9th century: animals,
people, wells, tents and camels, recorded in poetry and in the *anwāʾ* books of Arab philologists.

- **Source:** Stellarium's [Arabic (Ancient)](https://github.com/Stellarium/stellarium-skycultures/tree/master/arabic_ancient)
  sky culture by Khalid al-Ajaji, compiled from Ibn Qutaybah's *Kitāb al-Anwāʾ*, al-Ṣūfī and
  others (its references list the rest). Licence [CC BY-ND 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nd/4.0/).
- **How it's used:** every asterism and line exactly as published; only the stars' Hipparcos
  numbers are turned into positions ([d3-celestial](https://github.com/ofrohn/d3-celestial)'s star
  list, or [Hipparcos on VizieR](https://vizier.cds.unistra.fr/viz-bin/VizieR?-source=I/239)
  for fainter stars). The licence allows no changes, so nothing is trimmed or redrawn.
- **Why here:** Arabian tribes migrated west into the Maghreb from the 11th century (the Banu
  Hilal, and the Maʿqil into Morocco), bringing Bedouin culture with them.
- **Stories:** told asterism by asterism in the source's
  [description](https://github.com/Stellarium/stellarium-skycultures/blob/master/arabic_ancient/description.md).

## Tuareg

The Saharan Amazigh sky: a hunter, his dog, camels and a palm tree.

- **Source:** Henri Duveyrier, *Les Touareg du Nord* (Paris, 1864), pp. 423–426, from the Kel
  Ajjer and their caravan guides ([archive.org scan](https://archive.org/details/explorationdusa00cossgoog);
  public domain). Found through J. C. Holbrook, ["Celestial Women in Africa"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16647) (2020).
- **How it's used:** Duveyrier gives the names, their meanings and which stars they are (by
  constellation or Bayer letter; checked against the page scans, as the OCR garbles the
  Greek). He doesn't give lines, so **the lines joining each figure's stars are ours**
  ([`scripts/sky-tuareg.mjs`](../../../scripts/sky-tuareg.mjs)). Where he names a single star, it's
  drawn as nothing but named when you point at it. Names keep his French transcription.
- **Script:** the label shows each name in Tuareg Tifinagh, converted from Duveyrier's spelling
  for this sketch, as well as we could; **a Tamasheq reader should check it.** The method:
  only a word's first and last vowels are written, as in Tuareg practice (*a* as a dot,
  final *i* and *u* as their letters); his "dj" is ǧ (ⴶ), "ch" is š (ⵛ), "dh" is ḍ (ⴹ), "ou" is w
  or u; k and h use Unicode's Tuareg letters (ⴾ, ⵂ); doubled consonants are written once; the
  Arabic loan *El-Djema'at* drops its ʿayn, which Tuareg script lacks. Duveyrier's spellings stay
  alongside in `scripts/sky-tuareg.mjs`. Set in [Noto Sans Tifinagh Ghat](https://github.com/notofonts/tifinagh)
  (SIL Open Font License), the Tuareg letterforms of Ghat, the Kel Ajjer's town.
- **Left out:** Venus (*Tâtrit-tân-toufat*, the morning star) and the Milky Way (*Mâhellaou*),
  which aren't stars; and the girls *Tibaradîn* and the pool *Tesâhak* near Scorpius, which
  Duveyrier declined to place.

- **ⴰⵎⵏⵔ** Amanâr (He who opens): Orion, a hunter girded with his belt.
- **ⵜⴶⴱⵙⵜ ⵏ ⴰⵎⵏⵔ** Tâdjebest-en-Amanâr (The belt of Amanâr): Orion's belt.
- **ⴰⴷⵔ ⵏ ⴰⵍⴾⵓ** Adâr-n-elâkou (The foot in the mud): Rigel. In another telling Amanâr climbs out of a muddy
  well, and this is the last foot he pulls free, the last of his stars to rise; by then the jujube
  fruit has ripened and fallen.
- **ⴰⵢⴷⵉ** Eydi (The dog): Sirius, Amanâr's hunting dog, following him.
- **ⵉⵂⵏⴾⴹ** Ihenkâdh (The gazelles): Lepus, running ahead of the hunter.
- **ⴰⵡⵂⵎ** Aouhêm (The gazelle's young): β Canis Majoris.
- **ⵉⴼⵔⴾⴼⵔⴾⵏ** Ifarakfarâken (The flutter of a fan): ε, δ and η Canis Majoris; the word for the sound of a
  fan or a bird's wings, because violent winds blow when it appears.
- **ⵜⵍⵎⵜ** Tâlemt (The she-camel): Ursa Major.
- **ⴰⵡⵔⴰ** Aourâ (The camel calf): Ursa Minor.
- **ⵍⵎⴾⵛⵏ** Lemkechen (Hold!): Polaris, a woman told to hold the calf still while its mother is milked.
- **ⴰⵍⴶⵎⵜ** El-Djema'at (The assembly): ψ, λ, μ, ν, ξ Ursae Majoris, a triangle deliberating to kill
  Lemkechen; which is why she, terrified, never moves.
- **ⵛⵜ ⴰⵂⴹ** Chêt-Ahadh (The daughters of the night): the Pleiades. A verse names six sisters
  (Mâteredjrê, Erredjeâot, Mâteseksek, Essekâot, Mâtelarhlarh, Ellerhâot); the seventh is a boy's
  eye that flew into the sky. Which star is which isn't recorded.
- **ⵜⵣⵢⵜ** Tâzzeït (The palm tree), also **Tâzherdamt** (the scorpion): Scorpius.
- **ⴰⵎⵔⵜ** Amrôt (The young man in the palm): Antares, who began to climb the palm, saw girls in red
  coming from the pool, and stopped halfway up to watch them.
- **ⴾⴾⵢⴹ** Kôkoyyodh: Aldebaran.
- **ⵡⴷⵜ** Ouâdet: Canopus.
- **ⵜⵏⴼⵍⵜ** Tenâfelit (Wealth) and **ⵜⵣⵔⵜ** Tôzzert (Poverty): δ and ο Velorum (δ and ο Argus in 1864). Low in
  the south; crossing the Tanezrouft, guides steered by the midpoint of their rising and setting:
  due south.

## Maghrebi

The 28 lunar mansions (*manāzil al-qamar*), the stations the moon passes through in a month. The
dawn rising of each began a period of about 13 days (*nawʾ*), and the cycle ran the traditional
farming calendar across the Maghreb.

- **Source:** Stellarium's [Arabic (Lunar Stations)](https://github.com/Stellarium/stellarium-skycultures/tree/master/arabic_lunar_stations)
  sky culture by Khalid al-Ajaji. Licence [CC BY-ND 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nd/4.0/).
- **How it's used:** unaltered, as for Bedouin. Its 51 entries include the 28 mansions and other
  named asterisms the source shows with them (the Two Calves near Polaris, for one).
- **Naming:** the system is Arabian and was used across the Islamic world; it's labelled
  Maghrebi here for its place in the region's farming and travel almanacs. The source cites a
  poem on the mansions' stars attributed to Muḥammad al-Muqrī (1578–1638), published by
  Gustave-Adolphe de Calassanti-Motylinski in *Les mansions lunaires chez les Arabes* (1899).
- **Stories:** in the source's [description](https://github.com/Stellarium/stellarium-skycultures/blob/master/arabic_lunar_stations/description.md).
- **Script:** the Arabic names are set in Maghribi script, the Maghreb's own hand, with
  [Rabat](https://sourceforge.net/projects/maghribifonts/) (Maghribi Mabsout) by Youssef Jabri.
  Its licence: "May be used freely. No copying of glyphs or reverse engineering allowed without
  the written permission of the copyright owner", so it ships exactly as downloaded, unsubset.

## Greek

The 48 constellations Ptolemy catalogued in the *Almagest* (2nd century AD), the Greek sky that
al-Ṣūfī and later Europe built on.

- **Source:** the lines are [d3-celestial](https://github.com/ofrohn/d3-celestial)'s modern
  figures (BSD-3), for the 48 Ptolemaic constellations only.
- **How it's used:** Ptolemy's ship Argo appears as its three modern parts (Carina, Puppis,
  Vela), and Serpens in its two halves. The figures are modern drawings, not Ptolemy's; he
  described stars by their place in each figure, and drew no lines.
- **Glosses** below are general knowledge, written for this file.

- Andromeda: the princess chained to a rock for the sea monster, freed by Perseus
- Aquarius: the water-bearer, pouring from a jar
- Aquila: the eagle that carried Zeus's thunderbolts
- Ara: the altar where the gods swore their alliance against the Titans
- Aries: the ram of the golden fleece
- Auriga: the charioteer, holding a she-goat (Capella) and her kids
- Boötes: the herdsman, driving the bears round the pole
- Cancer: the crab that nipped Heracles as he fought the Hydra
- Canis Major: the greater dog, Orion's hound
- Canis Minor: the lesser dog
- Capricornus: the sea-goat
- Carina: the keel of Argo, the Argonauts' ship
- Cassiopeia: the vain queen, Andromeda's mother, on her throne
- Centaurus: the centaur, often Chiron the teacher
- Cepheus: the king, Andromeda's father
- Cetus: the sea monster sent against Andromeda
- Corona Austrina: the southern crown
- Corona Borealis: the northern crown, Ariadne's wedding crown
- Corvus: the crow, punished by Apollo for lying
- Crater: Apollo's cup
- Cygnus: the swan, Zeus in disguise
- Delphinus: the dolphin that found Amphitrite for Poseidon
- Draco: the dragon guarding the golden apples of the Hesperides
- Equuleus: the little horse
- Eridanus: the river
- Gemini: the twins Castor and Pollux
- Hercules: the hero Heracles (to Ptolemy, "the kneeler")
- Hydra: the water snake
- Leo: the Nemean lion killed by Heracles
- Lepus: the hare, at Orion's feet
- Libra: the scales (to Ptolemy, the scorpion's claws)
- Lupus: the wolf (to Ptolemy, a beast speared by the centaur)
- Lyra: the lyre of Orpheus
- Ophiuchus: the serpent-bearer, Asclepius the healer
- Orion: the hunter
- Pegasus: the winged horse
- Perseus: the hero who killed Medusa and freed Andromeda
- Pisces: two fish, Aphrodite and Eros escaping Typhon
- Piscis Austrinus: the southern fish, drinking Aquarius's stream
- Puppis: the stern of Argo
- Sagitta: the arrow
- Sagittarius: the archer, a centaur
- Scorpius: the scorpion that killed Orion
- Serpens Cauda: the serpent Ophiuchus holds, in two parts
- Taurus: the bull, Zeus carrying off Europa
- Triangulum: the triangle
- Ursa Major: the great bear, Callisto
- Ursa Minor: the little bear
- Vela: the sails of Argo
- Virgo: the maiden, holding an ear of wheat

## Western

The 88 constellations the International Astronomical Union fixed in 1922 (boundaries in 1930):
Ptolemy's 48 (Argo in three parts) plus figures added mostly by European navigators and
astronomers for the southern sky and the gaps between the old figures.

- **Source:** [d3-celestial](https://github.com/ofrohn/d3-celestial)'s lines and names (BSD-3).
- **How it's used:** all 88, unchanged.
- **The additions** (those not under Greek), with who added them: Pieter Keyser and Frederick de
  Houtman, Dutch navigators (1595–97); Petrus Plancius; Johannes Hevelius (1687); Nicolas-Louis
  de Lacaille, who mapped the southern sky from the Cape (1750s).
